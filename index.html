My background is a mix of teaching, operations, and psychological research. The projects here come from my work as an English as a second language (ESL) tutor.

I kept running into the same problem: students needed repeated practice, but the tools I had often made that practice feel too passive or mechanical. I knew that feeling from the other side too. As a student, I disliked learning that felt like going through the motions, and as a teacher, I hated becoming the person asking students to do it.

So I started building my own tools, usually games, to help students speak more, stay engaged, and repeat language in a way that felt natural. Everything here was built independently, tested in real lessons, and shaped by what students actually did with it.

I am now looking for work where I can keep improving my skills, solve meaningful problems, and be a part of making people's lives a little better.

Case Study GoPhish

Case Study

GoPhish

A familiar card game adapted into a real-time phonics tool, designed to make repeated sound practice feel natural and engadging.

Problem

A lot of online teaching ends up feeling passive by using worksheets, slides, and a lot of "teacher explains / student listens".
Students lose focus quickly, while teachers do not always have flexible tools that make practice feel interactive and keep everyone engaged.

Idea

Take a game students already know, Go Fish for example, and turn it into a learning tool. The goal was to make repetition feel natural, fun, and engaging.

Instead of solving worksheets, students would:

  • Play and compete
  • Repeat patterns without it feeling like an exercise

Built

I broke phonics into smaller groups, such as short vowels and digraphs, and turned each group into a card set. Each card includes four words, visuals, and one clear spelling pattern, so repetition happens naturally during gameplay but can still be used as a focused tool for teaching.

From there, I built a multiplayer web app where players can hold cards, organize their hand, pass cards, and complete sets. All actions sync in real time through Firebase.

Practice

The goal is to complete the most phonics sets out of all other players. Players ask each other for cards, pass cards back and forth, and complete matching phonics sets. Repeated requests create repetition without making practice feel repetitive.

Worked

  • Students stayed focused for full sessions
  • Repetition happened naturally
  • Vocabulary and spelling patterns stuck more reliably over time
  • The system scaled easily by swapping card sets in and out

Hard

  • Large hands and screen space: testing showed that as players collected more cards, it became harder to find, select, and manage specific cards without slowing the game down.
  • Usability for younger students: some actions needed stronger visual feedback, clearer selection states, and larger previews so students could understand what was happening without extra explanation.
  • Translating physical play online: physical card games rely on touch, visibility, and table layout, so the digital version needed extra feedback systems to replace those cues.

Not Yet

I explored replacing individual cards with shared boards and tokens. This would reduce visual clutter, but it would also completely change the feel of the game. I decided to validate the existing system first.

Learned

  • Test core assumptions before building around them
  • Translating a physical game online means redesigning the interaction, not copying it
  • Digital feedback has to replace the satisfaction of physical play
  • Screen space is not just a UI constraint; it shapes the learning system

Outcome

This became a reliable tool for phonics practice: higher participation, smoother repetition, and more consistent exposure to spelling patterns.

I-have An online game that gets students speaking quickly.

Case Study

I-have

A fast reaction game that gives the start of an online lesson momentum by turning simple speaking prompts into a shared card chain.

Problem

Online lessons can feel awkward, especially with younger or quieter students. Without early momentum, students hesitate to speak, energy stays low, and the rest of the lesson becomes harder to build.

Idea

Create something fast, simple, and competitive. The focus was to create an intuitive and compelling game for all levels that gets students speaking immediately. This helps break the ice and gives students a positive experience.

Built

I created a closed loop system of 24 cards: 8 different items, each repeated 3 times. Each card contains "I have [X]" and "Who has [Y]". Every card connects to another card, so the game can begin anywhere.

To bring this online, I built a shared real-time interface where both players can see all cards, cards snap into a central chain, and actions sync in real time.

Testing led me to add a tie-break mechanic for near-simultaneous plays, a chain-break state to prevent deadlocks, manual card arrangement, and clearer animations.

Practice

One player begins: "I have a lion." They reveal: "Who has a horse?" Players race to find the matching card. The first player to react discards their card and continues the chain.

Because the game rewards speed, students stay focused, speaking happens naturally, and mistakes do not interrupt the flow. Rounds usually last 2 to 3 minutes and can be repeated multiple times.

Worked

  • Students spoke almost immediately
  • Competition increased energy and removed awkwardness
  • Students often wanted to replay it

Hard

  • Real-time syncing: simultaneous player actions exposed timing and fairness issues, especially when two players tried to play a card at nearly the same moment.
  • Architecture: early technical decisions created delays in state updates, which made the battle mechanic feel less reliable and required rethinking the real-time interaction model.
  • Game-state edge cases: testing revealed moments where the chain could stall if the required card had already been played, so I added a clear visual state showing that any card could now be used.
  • Digital feedback: physical games naturally provide visual and tactile cues; online, these had to be replaced with animations, timing cues, and clearer interaction states.

Not Yet

  • Expand content significantly
  • Entire rebranding, including name and visual design

These are currently in the works.

Learned

  • Reaction-based games need feedback that feels immediate and decisive
  • Animation is not decoration; it helps players understand timing, conflict, and reward
  • Digital products need sensory cues to create a feeling of impact
  • Branding should be considered before too much interface work becomes fixed

Outcome

This became a reliable tool for lesson warmups: faster engagement, more speaking early on, and smoother lesson transitions.

Progressing A physical sentence-building card game that makes grammar practice feel more natural.

Case Study

Progressing

A sentence-building card game where students use visual structure, repetition, and gameplay to practice present progressive grammar.

Problem

Grammar is often taught through drills and explanations. For many students, that feels unnatural and is difficult to retain.

  • Students do not get enough meaningful repetition
  • When they do, it often feels disengaging
  • Students struggle to use English grammar correctly in real-life conversation

Idea

Instead of explaining grammar, I wanted students to use it naturally. I focused on present progressive because it is common, confusing for ESL students, and structured enough to turn into a repeatable system.

Built

I started with a simple sentence structure: subject, "to be", verb, "-ing" form, and time expression. Then I created roughly 20 sentences and broke them into modular cards.

Cards are color-coded by role, each card includes a visual, and guide cards show correct sentence structure. This allowed students to build correctly before fully understanding grammar rules.

Practice

The goal is to be the first player to score 5 points. Players earn points by completing sentences correctly while slowing their opponent down. Over multiple turns, players build sentences step by step.

To make gameplay engaging, I added turn-based gameplay, multiple action types, ability cards that let players disrupt each other or gain advantages, and a spelling layer where correctly modifying verbs earned bonus actions.

Players can steal cards, pull from discard piles, and earn double points. This creates a balance between building correctly, managing your hand, and reacting to your opponent.

Worked

  • Students formed correct sentences more independently
  • Repetition felt natural
  • Participation increased
  • Students began self-correcting

Hard

  • Complexity vs usability: every new mechanic made the game more interesting and gave players more freedom, but also increased complexity and drifted away from the original goal. Finding the right balance required repeated testing and simplification.
  • Hand management: larger hands created clutter and slowed decision-making. Through testing, I adjusted card counts and game flow to keep the experience focused and easy to follow.
  • Scoring systems: early versions used more detailed scoring, but testing showed that this created bulk by forcing players to run calculations and keep tabs. The scoring system was simplified to reduce friction.
  • Physical iteration speed: unlike digital projects, every major change required updating and reprinting materials, making it harder to test multiple variations quickly.

Not Yet

  • Expand into more complex sentence structures
  • Build a full tense system
  • Create a digital version

Learned

  • Simplicity is usually stronger than feature richness
  • Motivation changes how repetition feels
  • Interaction is just as important as content
  • Systems need natural endpoints

Outcome

This became a reliable tool for grammar practice: stronger sentence accuracy and better retention, more confidence using grammar in conversation, and higher engagement than traditional grammar exercises.

Re-Call A memory-based vocabulary game built around repetition and active recall.

Case Study

Re-Call

A vocabulary game where players build a growing spoken sequence, moving from recognition toward active recall.

Problem

Most vocabulary practice focuses on recognition: matching words to images, translation, or multiple-choice exercises. This helps in the short term, but students often struggle to quickly recall vocabulary during real conversations.

Idea

Turn vocabulary practice into a competitive memory game. Instead of matching pairs, players build a growing chain they need to remember and repeat. Everything stays within one category, which helps students build stronger connections between words.

Built

The system includes vocabulary cards with visuals, a growing sequence, and turn-based gameplay. I also added second chance cards and a visual legend.

The platform handles visual interactions, while speaking happens through live conversation.

Practice

The goal is to be the last player remaining. One player starts: "I would like some water." The next player continues: "I would like some water and sauce." The sequence continues growing until someone makes a mistake.

A single word might be repeated more than ten times in one round. Games usually last around 10 to 15 minutes.

Worked

  • High repetition without repetitive feeling
  • Better recall than simple matching games
  • Students stayed engaged
  • The visual legend helped weaker learners participate

Hard

  • Sequence accuracy: because the game relies on a growing sequence, even small inconsistencies in card order could disrupt gameplay. Maintaining an accurate game state required careful tracking of revealed cards and turn progression.
  • Player-state mapping: the system needed to consistently associate actions, cards, and turns with the correct player. Several iterations were required to ensure the interface reflected the actual game state.
  • Balancing support and challenge: the visual legend needed to help students participate without removing the memory challenge entirely. Finding that balance required testing with students of different ability levels.

Not Yet

  • Expand with more semantic categories
  • Create a fully synchronized online multiplayer version

The current system is designed for players to interact through a shared interface and handle validation naturally, so extra systems are not necessary yet.

Learned

  • Interface layout becomes easier when the game state is clearly defined
  • Turn changes, timing, and feedback need to be mapped as part of the design
  • Some physical learning activities become stronger when supported digitally
  • Animation works best when it clarifies what just happened and what happens next

Outcome

This became a reliable tool for learning and practicing vocabulary, speaking English in a structured way, and creating active participation and engagement.
