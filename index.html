My background is a mix of teaching, operations, and psychological research. The projects here come from my work as an English as a second language (ESL) tutor, while the research documentation comes from my work as an academic researcher.

I kept running into the same problem: students needed repeated practice, but the tools I had often made that practice feel too passive or mechanical. I knew that feeling from the other side too. As a student, I disliked learning that felt like going through the motions, and as a teacher, I hated becoming the person asking students to do it.

So I started building my own tools, usually games, to help students speak more, stay engaged, and repeat language in a way that felt natural. Everything here was built independently, tested in real lessons, and shaped by what students actually did with it.

I am now looking for work where I can keep improving my skills, solve meaningful problems, and be a part of making people's lives a little better.

Case Study

GoPhish

The Problem

The Problem

A lot of online teaching ends up feeling passive by using worksheets, slides, and a lot of "teacher explains / student listens".

Students lose focus quickly.

Teachers do not always have flexible tools that make practice feel interactive and keep everyone engaged.

The Idea

Take a game students already know, Go Fish for example, and turn it into a learning tool.

The goal was to make repetition feel natural, fun, and engaging.

Instead of solving worksheets, students would:

  • Play and compete.
  • Repeat patterns without it feeling like an exercise.

How I Built It

I broke phonics into smaller groups, such as short vowels and digraphs, and turned each group into a card set.

Each card includes four words, visuals, and one clear spelling pattern.

That way, repetition happens naturally during gameplay but can still be used as a focused tool for teaching.

From there, I built a multiplayer web app where players can hold cards, organize their hand, pass cards, and complete sets.

All actions sync in real time through Firebase.

What It Looks Like

The goal is to complete the most phonics sets out of all other players.

Players ask each other for cards, pass cards back and forth, and complete matching phonics sets.

Repeated requests create repetition without making practice feel repetitive.

What Works

  • Students stay focused for full sessions.
  • Repetition happens naturally.
  • Vocabulary and spelling patterns stick more reliably over time.
  • The system scales easily by swapping card sets in and out.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Large hands and screen space: testing showed that as players collected more cards, it became harder to find, select, and manage specific cards without slowing the game down. I added a larger card display to make these actions easier.
  • Usability for younger students: some actions needed stronger visual feedback, clearer selection states, and larger previews. Students needed to understand what was happening without extra explanation. An example of this is the large and prominent pause button.
  • Translating physical play online: physical card games rely on touch, visibility, and table layout. The digital version needed extra feedback systems to replace those cues. That is where animations became helpful.

What's In the Works

I am currently exploring replacing individual cards with shared boards and tokens.

This could reduce visual clutter, but it would also change the feel and design of the game.

It would require a different visual design, new code, and a redesign of most card elements, so I am treating it as a larger iteration rather than a small adjustment.

My Takeaways

  • Test core assumptions before building around them.
  • Translating a physical game online means redesigning the interaction, not copying it.
  • Digital feedback has to replace the satisfaction of physical play.
  • Screen space is not just a UI constraint; it shapes the learning system.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable tool for phonics practice.

It led to higher participation, smoother repetition, and more consistent exposure to spelling patterns.

Case Study

iHave

The Problem

Online lessons can feel awkward, especially with younger or quieter students.

Without early momentum:

  • Students hesitate to speak.
  • Energy stays low.
  • Lessons become harder to build.

The Idea

Create something fast, simple, and competitive.

The focus is to create an intuitive and compelling game for all levels that gets students speaking immediately.

This helps break the ice and gives students a positive experience.

How I Built It

I created a closed loop system of cards:

  • 24 total cards.
  • 8 different items.
  • Each repeated 3 times.

Each card contains:

  • "I have [X]".
  • "Who has [Y]".

The goal of the game is to be the first player to empty their hand.

Every card connects to another card, so the game can begin anywhere.

To bring this online, I built a shared real-time interface where:

  • Both players can see all cards.
  • Cards snap into a central chain.
  • Actions sync in real time.

I also added systems based on testing:

Tie-break mechanic

If two players act at the same time, a quick click battle determines who gets to play the card.

Chain break handling

Testing revealed situations where the game could stall because the required card had already been played.

To prevent deadlocks, I added a visual state that clearly communicated when any card could be played.

UI improvements

  • Manual card arrangement.
  • Animations.

What It Looks Like

One player begins:

"I have a lion."

They reveal:

"Who has a horse?"

Players race to find the matching card.

The first player to react discards their card and continues the chain.

Because the game rewards speed:

  • Students stay focused.
  • Speaking happens naturally.
  • Mistakes do not interrupt flow.

Rounds usually last 2-3 minutes and can be repeated multiple times.


What Works

  • Students speak almost immediately.
  • Competition increases energy and removes awkwardness.
  • Students often want to replay it.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Real-time syncing: simultaneous player actions exposed timing and fairness issues, especially when two players tried to play a card at nearly the same moment.
  • Architecture: early technical decisions created delays in state updates, which made the battle mechanic feel less reliable and required rethinking the real-time interaction model.
  • Game-state edge cases: testing revealed moments where the chain could stall if the required card had already been played, so I added a clear visual state showing that any card could now be used.
  • Digital feedback: physical games naturally provide visual and tactile cues; online, these had to be replaced with animations, timing cues, and clearer interaction states.

What's In the Works

I am currently rebranding the game as Links.

This is a full style and mechanical rebrand that will significantly expand the content and grammar principles covered by the game.

My Takeaways

  • Reaction-based games need feedback that feels immediate and decisive.
  • Animation is not decoration; it helps players understand timing, conflict, and reward.
  • Digital products need sensory cues to create a feeling of impact.
  • Branding should be considered before too much interface work becomes fixed.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable tool for breaking the ice and teaching simple grammar principles:

  • Faster engagement.
  • More speaking early on.
  • Smoother lesson transitions.
  • Very memorable and easy to refer back to.

Case Study

Progressing

The Problem

Grammar is often taught through drills and explanations.

For many students, that feels unnatural and is difficult to retain.

This results in:

  • Students do not get enough meaningful repetition.
  • When they do, it often feels disengaging.
  • Students struggle to use English grammar correctly in real life.

The Idea

Instead of explaining grammar, I wanted students to use it naturally.

I focused on present progressive ("I am learning") because it is common, confusing for ESL students, and structured enough to turn into a repeatable system.

The goal was to create a game where:

  • Repetition happens naturally.
  • Sentence structure is visible.
  • Students learn through use instead of memorization.

How I Built It

I started with a simple sentence structure:

  • Subject
  • "To be" (am/is/are)
  • Verb
  • "-ing" form
  • Time expression

Then I created roughly 20 sentences and broke them into modular cards.

To make the system easier to use:

  • Cards are color-coded by role.
  • Each card includes a visual.
  • Guide cards show correct sentence structure.

This allowed students to build correctly before fully understanding grammar rules.

To make gameplay engaging, I added:

  • Turn-based gameplay.
  • Multiple action types.
  • Ability cards that let players disrupt each other or gain advantages.

I also added a spelling layer where correctly modifying verbs earned bonus actions.

Most of this was refined through repeated testing.

What It Looks Like

The goal is to be the first player to score 5 points.

Players earn points by completing sentences correctly while slowing their opponent down.

Over multiple turns, players build sentences step by step:

  • "She"
  • "is"
  • "walk"
  • "ing"
  • "now"

Players can also:

  • Steal cards.
  • Pull from discard piles.
  • Earn double points.

This creates a balance between:

  • Building correctly.
  • Managing your hand.
  • Reacting to your opponent.

Over time, students begin to internalize the correct grammatical structure without relying on explanations.

What Works

  • Students form correct sentences more independently.
  • Repetition feels natural.
  • Participation increases.
  • Students begin self-correcting.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Complexity vs usability: every new mechanic made the game more interesting and gave players more freedom, but also increased complexity and drifted away from the original goal. Finding the right balance required repeated testing and simplification.
  • Hand management: larger hands created clutter and slowed decision-making. Through testing, I adjusted card counts and game flow to keep the experience focused and easy to follow.
  • Scoring systems: early versions used more detailed scoring, but testing showed that this created bulk by forcing players to run calculations and keep tabs. The scoring system was simplified to reduce friction.
  • Physical iteration speed: unlike digital projects, every major change required updating and reprinting materials, making it harder to test multiple variations quickly.

What's In the Works

I plan to build a fully digital version of this game.

I will also test expansions into more complex sentence structures and a fuller tense system, including simpler grammar systems such as "to be" and "to have".

My Takeaways

  • Simplicity is usually stronger than feature richness.
  • Motivation changes how repetition feels.
  • Interaction is just as important as content.
  • Systems need natural endpoints.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable tool for grammar practice:

  • Stronger sentence accuracy and better retention.
  • More confidence using grammar in conversation.
  • Higher engagement than traditional grammar exercises.

Case Study

Re-Call

The Problem

Most vocabulary practice focuses on recognition:

  • Matching words to images.
  • Translation.
  • Multiple-choice exercises.

This helps in the short term, but students often struggle to quickly recall vocabulary during real conversations.

The Idea

Turn vocabulary practice into a competitive memory game.

Instead of matching pairs, players build a growing chain they need to remember and repeat.

Everything stays within one category, for example food, which helps students build stronger connections between words.

The goal is simple:

  • Remember all items in the sequence in the correct order.
  • Add something new.

How I Built It

The system includes:

  • Vocabulary cards with visuals.
  • A growing sequence.
  • Turn-based gameplay.

I also added:

Second chance cards

These are shuffled into the deck and allow players to recover from a mistake when used.

Visual legend

This shows all possible vocabulary items and helps students learn new words.

The platform handles visual interactions, while speaking happens through live conversation.

What It Looks Like

The goal is to be the last player remaining.

One player starts:

"I would like some water."

The next player continues:

"I would like some water and sauce."

The sequence continues growing until someone makes a mistake.

This creates:

  • Repeated vocabulary exposure.
  • Active recall.
  • Constant verbal participation.

A single word might be repeated 10+ times in one round.

Games usually last around 10-15 minutes.

What Works

  • High repetition without a repetitive feeling.
  • Better recall than simple matching games.
  • Students stay engaged.
  • The visual legend helps weaker learners participate.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Sequence accuracy: because the game relies on a growing sequence, even small inconsistencies in card order could disrupt gameplay. Maintaining an accurate game state required careful tracking of revealed cards and turn progression.
  • Player-state mapping: the system needed to consistently associate actions, cards, and turns with the correct player. Several iterations were required to ensure the interface reflected the actual game state.
  • Balancing support and challenge: the visual legend needed to help students participate without removing the memory challenge entirely. Finding that balance required testing with students of different ability levels.

What's In the Works

I am going to build a fully synchronized online multiplayer version.

I will also expand the game with more semantic categories, such as common restaurant dishes, household nouns, and everyday items.

My Takeaways

  • Interface layout becomes easier when the game state is clearly defined.
  • Turn changes, timing, and feedback need to be mapped as part of the design.
  • Some physical learning activities become stronger when supported digitally.
  • Animation works best when it clarifies what just happened and what happens next.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable, fun, and effective way to:

  • Learn and practice vocabulary.
  • Speak English in a structured way.
  • Create active participation and engagement.

Brief Mindfulness Intervention and Access to Internal States Academic research

Research Summary

Brief Mindfulness Intervention and Access to Internal States

Research Question

Research Question

Difficulties accessing and interpreting internal states are thought to contribute to obsessive-compulsive symptoms and related difficulties in self-regulation. This study investigated whether a brief mindfulness-based intervention could temporarily improve access to internal bodily states in individuals with elevated obsessive-compulsive tendencies.

My Role

  • Lead researcher
  • Study design and implementation
  • Participant recruitment and coordination
  • Statistical analysis
  • Lead author

Method

63 students with elevated obsessive-compulsive symptoms were randomly assigned to either a brief mindfulness meditation condition or a neutral audio condition.

Participants then completed a muscle-tension reproduction task designed to measure access to internal bodily states.

Findings & Conclusions

  • Participants who completed the mindfulness intervention demonstrated more accurate muscle-tension reproduction
  • The intervention improved access to internal bodily states compared to the control condition
  • Even a brief mindfulness exercise produced measurable cognitive effects

The findings suggest that access to internal states can be temporarily improved through mindfulness-based interventions and support theories linking mindfulness to improved self-awareness and self-regulation.

Publication: In press

Positive Inner Speech Training and Performance Anxiety Academic research

Research Summary

Positive Inner Speech Training and Performance Anxiety

Research Question

Research Question

Many students experience anxiety before academic exams, which can negatively affect performance. While spontaneous inner speech has been linked to emotional regulation, it was unclear whether deliberately training positive inner speech could reduce exam-related performance anxiety.

My Role

  • Lead researcher
  • Study design and implementation
  • Participant recruitment and coordination
  • Statistical analysis
  • Lead author

Method

101 students completed measures of performance anxiety and inner speech before being randomly assigned to either a positive inner speech training condition or a neutral control condition.

Participants completed three consecutive days of training before repeating the anxiety assessment the evening before major exams.

Findings & Conclusions

  • Positive inner speech training reduced cognitive anxiety
  • Positive inner speech training increased self-confidence
  • Neutral inner speech training produced substantially smaller changes
  • Training effectiveness was predicted by participants' existing inner speech habits

The findings suggest that positive inner speech training may be an effective intervention for reducing academic performance anxiety and that individual differences in spontaneous inner speech influence intervention outcomes.

Publication: In press

The Hebrew VISQ-R: Measuring Inner Speech in Hebrew Speakers Academic research

Research Summary

The Hebrew VISQ-R: Measuring Inner Speech in Hebrew Speakers

Research Question

Research Question

Inner speech plays an important role in self-regulation, planning, and reflection, yet most tools used to measure it were developed for English-speaking populations. Without a validated Hebrew version of the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire-Revised, researchers lacked a reliable way to investigate inner speech among Hebrew speakers and compare findings across cultures.

My Role

  • Assisted with questionnaire translation
  • Assisted with participant recruitment and data collection
  • Conducted statistical analyses

Method

The project focused on adapting and validating a Hebrew version of the VISQ-R. Data were collected from 406 participants, with a subset completing additional executive-function measures.

The study evaluated factor structure using Confirmatory Factor Analysis, internal reliability across all subscales, and relationships between inner speech and executive functioning.

Findings & Conclusions

  • The Hebrew VISQ-R successfully replicated the original five-factor structure
  • All subscales demonstrated acceptable reliability
  • Several forms of inner speech were associated with executive-function measures
  • The questionnaire demonstrated good convergent and divergent validity

The findings support the Hebrew VISQ-R as a reliable research instrument and provide further evidence linking specific varieties of inner speech to executive functioning.

Publication: Frontiers in Psychology (2022), "The Psychometric Properties of the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire-Revised in Hebrew"
