My background combines education, operations management, and psychological research. The work here comes from two parts of that experience: learning tools I designed and tested as an English as a second language (ESL) tutor, and academic research that I led or contributed to.

Many of the learning projects began with a practical problem. Students needed repeated practice, but the available tools often made that practice feel passive or mechanical. I knew that feeling from the other side too. As a student, I disliked learning that felt like going through the motions, and as a teacher, I hated becoming the person asking students to do it.

So I began building my own tools, usually games, to encourage participation, communication, and meaningful repetition. They were built independently, tested in real lessons, and shaped by how students actually used them.

The research projects explored different questions, including how psychological interventions affect anxiety, self-confidence, and awareness of internal states. The subject matter was different, but the underlying process was much the same: define the problem, develop an approach, test it, examine the evidence, and refine what we understand.

I am now looking for work where I can apply and continue developing this approach, solve meaningful problems, and contribute to products, services, or systems that make people's lives a little better.

Case Study iHave
01Problem 02Process 03Solution 04Challenges 05Learnings 06Future

01The Problem

Building rapport and creating comfortable communication is often more difficult online than in person, particularly with younger or quieter students. Without that connection, students are less likely to participate, take risks, or engage fully in the lesson.

I wanted to explore whether a simple competitive activity could reduce these barriers and encourage students to interact more naturally.

02Process

I began with a simple design goal:

Create a game that requires almost no explanation but encourages students to speak from the moment it begins.

Rather than designing a new game from scratch, I adapted the familiar "I Have / Who Has" activity into a fast-paced multiplayer experience.

The system uses a closed loop of interconnected cards. Players race to identify the next matching card and continue the chain, creating a game that rewards attention, speed, and participation rather than strategy or prior knowledge.

The initial version was tested during live lessons and refined through observation and iteration.

03Solution

The current version is a real-time multiplayer web application where players compete to empty their hand by correctly linking cards into a shared chain.

The game includes:

  • Real-time multiplayer synchronization
  • Shared play area visible to both players
  • Automatic chain building
  • Tie-break mechanic for simultaneous plays
  • Visual handling of chain-break scenarios
  • Manual card arrangement before each round
  • Animations and interaction feedback

Rounds typically last two to three minutes and can be repeated multiple times within a lesson.

By keeping the rules simple and the pace fast, the game encourages students to focus on interaction rather than correctness, helping communication develop naturally.

04Challenges & Iteration

One of the main challenges was managing situations where both players attempted to act at the same time.

Testing revealed fairness issues whenever two players tried to play a card simultaneously. Rather than preventing these moments, I introduced a tie-break mechanic that turned them into part of the game experience.

Additional testing uncovered situations where gameplay could stall because the required card had already been played. To address this, I added a clear visual state that communicates when any card can be used to continue the chain.

The project also highlighted how many social and visual cues from physical activities need to be recreated when interactions move online.

05What I Learned

  • Creating opportunities for interaction can be more important than increasing educational complexity.
  • Simple systems are often easier to learn, replay, and enjoy.
  • Real-time multiplayer interactions require clear feedback when players act simultaneously.
  • Visual feedback helps replace cues that are naturally present in physical activities.
  • Designing for social interaction can be just as important as designing for learning outcomes.

06Future Direction

The project is currently being rebranded as Links.

Future development focuses on expanding the range of vocabulary and grammar patterns supported by the system while preserving the fast-paced interaction that makes it effective.

The rebrand also provides an opportunity to refine the visual identity and create a stronger foundation for future content expansion.

Case Study Progressing
01Problem 02Process 03Solution 04Challenges 05Learnings 06Future

01The Problem

Grammar is often taught through explanations and repetitive drills. While these approaches introduce grammatical rules, they rarely give students enough meaningful practice to use them confidently in conversation.

I wanted to explore whether grammar could be learned through play rather than memorization, allowing students to build correct sentences repeatedly without the activity feeling repetitive.

02Process

I chose the present progressive because it is both common and challenging for many ESL learners while following a structure that could be broken into clear, reusable components.

If grammar practice could be gamified, repeated sentence building could feel more natural, competitive, and enjoyable, giving students the repetition they need over time.

Rather than adapting an existing game, I designed an original system built around the structure of a sentence itself.

Each sentence was divided into modular cards representing subject, "to be", verb, "-ing" ending, and time expression.

Colour coding, illustrations, and guide cards made each grammatical role immediately visible, allowing students to build correct sentences before fully understanding the underlying rules.

To encourage repeated practice, I introduced turn-based gameplay, player interaction, and ability cards that rewarded planning without distracting from the learning objective.

The game evolved through repeated classroom testing, with mechanics added, removed, and simplified based on how students actually played.

03Solution

The current physical game challenges players to be the first to score five points by building complete grammatical sentences while responding to their opponent's actions.

Key features include:

  • Modular sentence construction
  • Colour-coded grammar roles
  • Guide cards for independent play
  • Strategic ability cards
  • Simple scoring system
  • Bonus actions for correct spelling changes

Instead of separating grammar explanations from practice, the game embeds repetition directly into play, encouraging students to internalize grammatical structures through repeated use.

Ability cards add moments of choice and interaction, but they only work when they reinforce the sentence-building loop rather than compete with it.

04Challenges & Iteration

The biggest challenge was balancing strategic depth with accessibility.

Each new mechanic made the game more interesting, but also increased complexity and pulled attention away from the original learning goal. Repeated testing led to several rounds of simplification, removing mechanics that added complexity without improving the experience.

Testing also highlighted practical issues with hand management and scoring. Larger hands slowed decision-making, while more detailed scoring systems interrupted the flow of play. Both were simplified to keep the game focused and intuitive.

Cards that created useful decisions stayed in the system. Cards that slowed the game down or pulled attention away from sentence building were removed or simplified.

Because the project is physical, every significant change required redesigning, printing, and rebuilding components before testing could continue.

05What I Learned

  • Simplicity is often more valuable than feature richness.
  • Motivation changes how repetition is experienced.
  • Well-designed interaction can make difficult concepts feel approachable.
  • Not every mechanic has to support the core learning objective.

The most useful mechanics were the ones that made students want to keep using the structure, not the ones that made the game feel more complicated.

06Future Direction

The next stage of the project is a digital implementation that preserves the core gameplay while making it easier to iterate, test, and expand.

The underlying system is designed to support additional grammar topics, including other verb tenses and simpler sentence structures such as "to be" and "to have."

Case Study Re-Call
01Problem 02Process 03Solution 04Challenges 05Learnings 06Future

01The Problem

Most vocabulary activities focus on recognition by asking learners to match words, translate them, or choose the correct answer. While these approaches support short-term learning, they do not always prepare students to retrieve vocabulary quickly during real conversations.

I wanted to explore whether vocabulary practice could focus on active recall instead, encouraging students to repeatedly retrieve words rather than simply recognize them.

02Process

I adapted the familiar structure of a memory game into a system built around retrieval rather than matching.

Instead of finding pairs, players build a growing spoken sequence that must be remembered and repeated in the correct order before adding a new vocabulary item.

Keeping every round within a single semantic category, such as food, encourages stronger connections between related words while naturally increasing repetition.

The system was refined through testing, balancing challenge with enough support to keep students engaged.

03Solution

Players take turns recalling and extending a shared vocabulary sequence, with each correct response increasing the length of the chain.

The game includes:

  • Category-based vocabulary sets
  • Progressive sequence building
  • Second Chance cards
  • Optional visual vocabulary reference
  • Turn-based gameplay
  • Spoken interaction supported by a digital interface

A single vocabulary item may be recalled many times during a round, creating meaningful repetition without the experience feeling repetitive.

The visual reference gives students a way back into the activity when they need support, without turning the game into a recognition-only task.

04Challenges & Iteration

The biggest challenge was maintaining a clear and reliable game state as the sequence grew.

Because every turn depends on the accuracy of the previous one, even small inconsistencies could interrupt gameplay. Several iterations focused on improving how player turns, revealed cards, and sequence progression were represented.

Another challenge involved balancing support with difficulty. The visual reference needed to help students participate without removing the memory challenge that gives the activity its value.

The project also reinforced the importance of using interface feedback to clearly communicate what happened, whose turn it is, and what comes next.

05What I Learned

  • Active recall develops differently from simple recognition.
  • Repetition becomes more engaging when learners have a meaningful objective.
  • Clear game states make both interface design and user experience significantly simpler.
  • Well-designed support should increase confidence without removing challenge.
  • Digital tools work best when they strengthen, rather than replace, spoken interaction.

06Future Direction

Future development focuses on a fully synchronized multiplayer version that allows players to participate remotely while preserving the same conversational experience.

The system is also designed to support additional semantic categories and more advanced vocabulary as learners progress.

Research Summary Brief Mindfulness Intervention and Access to Internal States
01Question 02My Role 03Method 04Findings

01Question

Difficulties accessing and interpreting internal states are thought to contribute to obsessive-compulsive symptoms and related difficulties in self-regulation. This study investigated whether a brief mindfulness-based intervention could temporarily improve access to internal bodily states in individuals with elevated obsessive-compulsive tendencies.

02My Role

  • Lead researcher
  • Study design and implementation
  • Participant recruitment and coordination
  • Statistical analysis
  • Lead author

03Method

63 students with elevated obsessive-compulsive symptoms were randomly assigned to either a brief mindfulness meditation condition or a neutral audio condition.

Participants then completed a muscle-tension reproduction task designed to measure access to internal bodily states.

04Findings

  • Participants who completed the mindfulness intervention demonstrated more accurate muscle-tension reproduction.
  • The intervention improved access to internal bodily states compared to the control condition.
  • Even a brief mindfulness exercise produced measurable cognitive effects.

Research Summary Positive Inner Speech Training and Performance Anxiety
01Question 02My Role 03Method 04Findings

01Question

Many students experience anxiety before academic exams, which can negatively affect performance. While spontaneous inner speech has been linked to emotional regulation, it was unclear whether deliberately training positive inner speech could reduce exam-related performance anxiety.

02My Role

  • Lead researcher
  • Study design and implementation
  • Participant recruitment and coordination
  • Statistical analysis
  • Lead author

03Method

101 students completed measures of performance anxiety and inner speech before being randomly assigned to either a positive inner speech training condition or a neutral control condition.

Participants completed three consecutive days of training before repeating the anxiety assessment the evening before major exams.

04Findings

  • Positive inner speech training reduced cognitive anxiety.
  • Positive inner speech training increased self-confidence.
  • Neutral inner speech training produced substantially smaller changes.
  • Training effectiveness was predicted by participants' existing inner speech habits.

The Hebrew VISQ-R: Measuring Inner Speech in Hebrew Speakers

A validation study adapting an inner-speech questionnaire for Hebrew-speaking participants.

Publication: Frontiers in Psychology (2023), "The Psychometric Properties of the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire-Revised in Hebrew"
Read full research summary Next section

Conclusion

Validated Hebrew measure

The Hebrew VISQ-R functions as a reliable research instrument for Hebrew-speaking participants.

Research Summary The Hebrew VISQ-R: Measuring Inner Speech in Hebrew Speakers
01Question 02My Role 03Method 04Findings

01Question

Inner speech plays an important role in self-regulation, planning, and reflection, yet most tools used to measure it were developed for English-speaking populations. Without a validated Hebrew version of the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire-Revised, researchers lacked a reliable way to investigate inner speech among Hebrew speakers and compare findings across cultures.

02My Role

  • Assisted with questionnaire translation
  • Assisted with participant recruitment and data collection
  • Conducted statistical analyses

03Method

The project focused on adapting and validating a Hebrew version of the VISQ-R. Data were collected from 406 participants, with a subset completing additional executive-function measures.

The study evaluated factor structure using Confirmatory Factor Analysis, internal reliability across all subscales, and relationships between inner speech and executive functioning.

04Findings

  • The Hebrew VISQ-R successfully replicated the original five-factor structure.
  • All subscales demonstrated acceptable reliability.
  • Several forms of inner speech were associated with executive-function measures.
  • The questionnaire demonstrated good convergent and divergent validity.

Case Study GoPhish
01Problem 02Process 03Solution 04Challenges 05Learnings 06Future

01The Problem

Traditional phonics practice is often repetitive and passive, making it difficult to maintain engagement during online lessons. While physical card games can create interaction and competition, they can't be used remotely.

I wanted to explore whether a familiar game structure could be adapted into a digital format while preserving the interaction that makes learning activities engaging.

02Process

I began with a simple hypothesis:

If phonics practice is embedded within a competitive multiplayer game, students will engage more actively and receive more meaningful repetition.

Rather than building a large content library immediately, I focused on validating the core gameplay loop first.

I adapted the structure of Go Fish into a system built around phonics patterns, creating a small set of cards covering short vowels and common digraphs. The initial version was tested during live lessons and expanded only after the core interaction proved effective.

03Solution

The current version is a real-time multiplayer web application that allows two players to exchange cards, collect matching phonics sets, and compete to complete groups of related spelling patterns.

It includes:

  • Real-time multiplayer synchronization using Firebase
  • Teacher-controlled content selection
  • Customizable rules and turn structure
  • Manual card organization
  • Visual support through illustrated cards
  • Set-completion and scoring mechanics

Instead of practicing sounds through isolated drills, students repeatedly encounter target patterns while pursuing a game objective.

04Challenges & Iteration

One of the main challenges was adapting a physical card game to a digital environment.

As the number of cards increased, it became harder for players to organize and view their hands. This led to several interface revisions, including expandable hand views, manual card organization, and clearer visual feedback for selected cards.

Testing also highlighted how much physical games rely on spatial organization. Some interactions that feel natural with physical cards required additional interface support when moved online.

05What I Learned

  • Engagement is driven by interaction and competition.
  • Using a familiar game structure reduces the amount of explanation students need before playing.
  • Small interface changes, particularly hand organization and selection feedback, substantially improve usability.
  • Testing the core gameplay loop before expanding the card library prevents unnecessary work.
  • Translating a physical game into digital format requires redesigning feedback and organization.

06Future Direction

As more content was added, it became clear that the card-based approach created increasing visual complexity.

One direction I continue to explore is replacing individual cards with a shared board-based system that preserves the learning goals while reducing screen clutter.

The project also provides a foundation for expanding into more advanced spelling patterns and reading concepts.

Case Study

GoPhish

The Problem

The Problem

A lot of online teaching ends up feeling passive by using worksheets, slides, and a lot of "teacher explains / student listens".

Students lose focus quickly.

Teachers do not always have flexible tools that make practice feel interactive and keep everyone engaged.

The Idea

Take a game students already know, Go Fish for example, and turn it into a learning tool.

The goal was to make repetition feel natural, fun, and engaging.

Instead of solving worksheets, students would:

  • Play and compete.
  • Repeat patterns without it feeling like an exercise.

How I Built It

I broke phonics into smaller groups, such as short vowels and digraphs, and turned each group into a card set.

Each card includes four words, visuals, and one clear spelling pattern.

That way, repetition happens naturally during gameplay but can still be used as a focused tool for teaching.

From there, I built a multiplayer web app where players can hold cards, organize their hand, pass cards, and complete sets.

All actions sync in real time through Firebase.

What It Looks Like

The goal is to complete the most phonics sets out of all other players.

Players ask each other for cards, pass cards back and forth, and complete matching phonics sets.

Repeated requests create repetition without making practice feel repetitive.

What Works

  • Students stay focused for full sessions.
  • Repetition happens naturally.
  • Vocabulary and spelling patterns stick more reliably over time.
  • The system scales easily by swapping card sets in and out.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Large hands and screen space: testing showed that as players collected more cards, it became harder to find, select, and manage specific cards without slowing the game down. I added a larger card display to make these actions easier.
  • Usability for younger students: some actions needed stronger visual feedback, clearer selection states, and larger previews. Students needed to understand what was happening without extra explanation. An example of this is the large and prominent pause button.
  • Translating physical play online: physical card games rely on touch, visibility, and table layout. The digital version needed extra feedback systems to replace those cues. That is where animations became helpful.

What's In the Works

I am currently exploring replacing individual cards with shared boards and tokens.

This could reduce visual clutter, but it would also change the feel and design of the game.

It would require a different visual design, new code, and a redesign of most card elements, so I am treating it as a larger iteration rather than a small adjustment.

My Takeaways

  • Test core assumptions before building around them.
  • Translating a physical game online means redesigning the interaction, not copying it.
  • Digital feedback has to replace the satisfaction of physical play.
  • Screen space is not just a UI constraint; it shapes the learning system.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable tool for phonics practice.

It led to higher participation, smoother repetition, and more consistent exposure to spelling patterns.

Case Study

iHave

The Problem

Online lessons can feel awkward, especially with younger or quieter students.

Without early momentum:

  • Students hesitate to speak.
  • Energy stays low.
  • Lessons become harder to build.

The Idea

Create something fast, simple, and competitive.

The focus is to create an intuitive and compelling game for all levels that gets students speaking immediately.

This helps break the ice and gives students a positive experience.

How I Built It

I created a closed loop system of cards:

  • 24 total cards.
  • 8 different items.
  • Each repeated 3 times.

Each card contains:

  • "I have [X]".
  • "Who has [Y]".

The goal of the game is to be the first player to empty their hand.

Every card connects to another card, so the game can begin anywhere.

To bring this online, I built a shared real-time interface where:

  • Both players can see all cards.
  • Cards snap into a central chain.
  • Actions sync in real time.

I also added systems based on testing:

Tie-break mechanic

If two players act at the same time, a quick click battle determines who gets to play the card.

Chain break handling

Testing revealed situations where the game could stall because the required card had already been played.

To prevent deadlocks, I added a visual state that clearly communicated when any card could be played.

UI improvements

  • Manual card arrangement.
  • Animations.

What It Looks Like

One player begins:

"I have a lion."

They reveal:

"Who has a horse?"

Players race to find the matching card.

The first player to react discards their card and continues the chain.

Because the game rewards speed:

  • Students stay focused.
  • Speaking happens naturally.
  • Mistakes do not interrupt flow.

Rounds usually last 2-3 minutes and can be repeated multiple times.


What Works

  • Students speak almost immediately.
  • Competition increases energy and removes awkwardness.
  • Students often want to replay it.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Real-time syncing: simultaneous player actions exposed timing and fairness issues, especially when two players tried to play a card at nearly the same moment.
  • Architecture: early technical decisions created delays in state updates, which made the battle mechanic feel less reliable and required rethinking the real-time interaction model.
  • Game-state edge cases: testing revealed moments where the chain could stall if the required card had already been played, so I added a clear visual state showing that any card could now be used.
  • Digital feedback: physical games naturally provide visual and tactile cues; online, these had to be replaced with animations, timing cues, and clearer interaction states.

What's In the Works

I am currently rebranding the game as Links.

This is a full style and mechanical rebrand that will significantly expand the content and grammar principles covered by the game.

My Takeaways

  • Reaction-based games need feedback that feels immediate and decisive.
  • Animation is not decoration; it helps players understand timing, conflict, and reward.
  • Digital products need sensory cues to create a feeling of impact.
  • Branding should be considered before too much interface work becomes fixed.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable tool for breaking the ice and teaching simple grammar principles:

  • Faster engagement.
  • More speaking early on.
  • Smoother lesson transitions.
  • Very memorable and easy to refer back to.

Case Study

Progressing

The Problem

Grammar is often taught through drills and explanations.

For many students, that feels unnatural and is difficult to retain.

This results in:

  • Students do not get enough meaningful repetition.
  • When they do, it often feels disengaging.
  • Students struggle to use English grammar correctly in real life.

The Idea

Instead of explaining grammar, I wanted students to use it naturally.

I focused on present progressive ("I am learning") because it is common, confusing for ESL students, and structured enough to turn into a repeatable system.

The goal was to create a game where:

  • Repetition happens naturally.
  • Sentence structure is visible.
  • Students learn through use instead of memorization.

How I Built It

I started with a simple sentence structure:

  • Subject
  • "To be" (am/is/are)
  • Verb
  • "-ing" form
  • Time expression

Then I created roughly 20 sentences and broke them into modular cards.

To make the system easier to use:

  • Cards are color-coded by role.
  • Each card includes a visual.
  • Guide cards show correct sentence structure.

This allowed students to build correctly before fully understanding grammar rules.

To make gameplay engaging, I added:

  • Turn-based gameplay.
  • Multiple action types.
  • Ability cards that let players disrupt each other or gain advantages.

I also added a spelling layer where correctly modifying verbs earned bonus actions.

Most of this was refined through repeated testing.

What It Looks Like

The goal is to be the first player to score 5 points.

Players earn points by completing sentences correctly while slowing their opponent down.

Over multiple turns, players build sentences step by step:

  • "She"
  • "is"
  • "walk"
  • "ing"
  • "now"

Players can also:

  • Steal cards.
  • Pull from discard piles.
  • Earn double points.

This creates a balance between:

  • Building correctly.
  • Managing your hand.
  • Reacting to your opponent.

Over time, students begin to internalize the correct grammatical structure without relying on explanations.

What Works

  • Students form correct sentences more independently.
  • Repetition feels natural.
  • Participation increases.
  • Students begin self-correcting.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Complexity vs usability: every new mechanic made the game more interesting and gave players more freedom, but also increased complexity and drifted away from the original goal. Finding the right balance required repeated testing and simplification.
  • Hand management: larger hands created clutter and slowed decision-making. Through testing, I adjusted card counts and game flow to keep the experience focused and easy to follow.
  • Scoring systems: early versions used more detailed scoring, but testing showed that this created bulk by forcing players to run calculations and keep tabs. The scoring system was simplified to reduce friction.
  • Physical iteration speed: unlike digital projects, every major change required updating and reprinting materials, making it harder to test multiple variations quickly.

What's In the Works

I plan to build a fully digital version of this game.

I will also test expansions into more complex sentence structures and a fuller tense system, including simpler grammar systems such as "to be" and "to have".

My Takeaways

  • Simplicity is usually stronger than feature richness.
  • Motivation changes how repetition feels.
  • Interaction is just as important as content.
  • Systems need natural endpoints.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable tool for grammar practice:

  • Stronger sentence accuracy and better retention.
  • More confidence using grammar in conversation.
  • Higher engagement than traditional grammar exercises.

Re-Call

The Problem

Most vocabulary practice focuses on recognition:

  • Matching words to images.
  • Translation.
  • Multiple-choice exercises.

This helps in the short term, but students often struggle to quickly recall vocabulary during real conversations.

The Idea

Turn vocabulary practice into a competitive memory game.

Instead of matching pairs, players build a growing chain they need to remember and repeat.

Everything stays within one category, for example food, which helps students build stronger connections between words.

The goal is simple:

  • Remember all items in the sequence in the correct order.
  • Add something new.

How I Built It

The system includes:

  • Vocabulary cards with visuals.
  • A growing sequence.
  • Turn-based gameplay.

I also added:

Second chance cards

These are shuffled into the deck and allow players to recover from a mistake when used.

Visual legend

This shows all possible vocabulary items and helps students learn new words.

The platform handles visual interactions, while speaking happens through live conversation.

What It Looks Like

The goal is to be the last player remaining.

One player starts:

"I would like some water."

The next player continues:

"I would like some water and sauce."

The sequence continues growing until someone makes a mistake.

This creates:

  • Repeated vocabulary exposure.
  • Active recall.
  • Constant verbal participation.

A single word might be repeated 10+ times in one round.

Games usually last around 10-15 minutes.

What Works

  • High repetition without a repetitive feeling.
  • Better recall than simple matching games.
  • Students stay engaged.
  • The visual legend helps weaker learners participate.

Challenges and Refinements

  • Sequence accuracy: because the game relies on a growing sequence, even small inconsistencies in card order could disrupt gameplay. Maintaining an accurate game state required careful tracking of revealed cards and turn progression.
  • Player-state mapping: the system needed to consistently associate actions, cards, and turns with the correct player. Several iterations were required to ensure the interface reflected the actual game state.
  • Balancing support and challenge: the visual legend needed to help students participate without removing the memory challenge entirely. Finding that balance required testing with students of different ability levels.

What's In the Works

I am going to build a fully synchronized online multiplayer version.

I will also expand the game with more semantic categories, such as common restaurant dishes, household nouns, and everyday items.

My Takeaways

  • Interface layout becomes easier when the game state is clearly defined.
  • Turn changes, timing, and feedback need to be mapped as part of the design.
  • Some physical learning activities become stronger when supported digitally.
  • Animation works best when it clarifies what just happened and what happens next.

The Outcomes

This became a reliable, fun, and effective way to:

  • Learn and practice vocabulary.
  • Speak English in a structured way.
  • Create active participation and engagement.

Research Question

Difficulties accessing and interpreting internal states are thought to contribute to obsessive-compulsive symptoms and related difficulties in self-regulation.   • Statistical analysis
  • Lead author

Method

63 students with elevated obsessive-compulsive symptoms were randomly assigned to either a brief mindfulness meditation condition or a neutral audio condition.

Research Summary

Positive Inner Speech Training and Performance Anxiety

Research Question

Research Question

Many students experience anxiety before academic exams, which can negatively affect performance. While spontaneous inner speech has been linked to emotional regulation, it was unclear whether deliberately training positive inner speech could reduce exam-related performance anxiety.

My Role

  • Lead researcher
  • Study design and implementation
  • Participant recruitment and coordination
  • Statistical analysis
  • Lead author

Method

101 students completed measures of performance anxiety and inner speech before being randomly assigned to either a positive inner speech training condition or a neutral control condition.

Participants completed three consecutive days of training before repeating the anxiety assessment the evening before major exams.

Findings

  • Positive inner speech training reduced cognitive anxiety
  • Positive inner speech training increased self-confidence
  • Neutral inner speech training produced substantially smaller changes
  • Training effectiveness was predicted by participants' existing inner speech habits

The findings suggest that positive inner speech training may be an effective intervention for reducing academic performance anxiety and that individual differences in spontaneous inner speech influence intervention outcomes.

Publication: In press

The Hebrew VISQ-R: Measuring Inner Speech in Hebrew Speakers Academic research

Research Summary

The Hebrew VISQ-R: Measuring Inner Speech in Hebrew Speakers

Research Question

Research Question

Inner speech plays an important role in self-regulation, planning, and reflection, yet most tools used to measure it were developed for English-speaking populations. Without a validated Hebrew version of the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire-Revised, researchers lacked a reliable way to investigate inner speech among Hebrew speakers and compare findings across cultures.

My Role

  • Assisted with questionnaire translation
  • Assisted with participant recruitment and data collection
  • Conducted statistical analyses

Method

The project focused on adapting and validating a Hebrew version of the VISQ-R. Data were collected from 406 participants, with a subset completing additional executive-function measures.

The study evaluated factor structure using Confirmatory Factor Analysis, internal reliability across all subscales, and relationships between inner speech and executive functioning.

Findings

  • The Hebrew VISQ-R successfully replicated the original five-factor structure
  • All subscales demonstrated acceptable reliability
  • Several forms of inner speech were associated with executive-function measures
  • The questionnaire demonstrated good convergent and divergent validity

The findings support the Hebrew VISQ-R as a reliable research instrument and provide further evidence linking specific varieties of inner speech to executive functioning.

Publication: Frontiers in Psychology (2023), "The Psychometric Properties of the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire-Revised in Hebrew"
